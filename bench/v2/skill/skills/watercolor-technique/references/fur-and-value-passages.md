# Fur and value passages

A passage is a wash or group of marks with one job.

Use this review alongside [fur, facial values and selective masking](fur-value-masking.md). It separates broad form from later texture.

1. Block in the main shapes: locate the silhouette, overlaps and connected light and shadow groups. A recognizable low-detail study should precede fine fur.
2. Assign the lights: distinguish bare paper, pale local color, protected strands and areas where paint can still be removed. Preserve the intended width and contrast, not merely the brightest pixel.
3. Choose how the fur flows: record growth direction, strand curvature and group size by region. Brush pressure and stroke length should follow that structure.
4. Choose what each edge should do: keep defining facial landmarks distinct, allow selected soft transitions, and use broken edges where the reference shows them.
5. Prepare the material state: choose water, pigment concentration, brush width and how the brush meets the paper separately. Check paper moisture across the area being painted and nearby wet areas.
6. Review the actual stage: inspect opening drawing, first values, dried layers and texture. Correct a misplaced shape before adding small marks.

[David Cox's outline and tint instructions](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/65962/pg65962-images.html#ON_OUTLINE) support clear general form, prepared mixtures and deliberate shadow masses. His illustrations teach value relationships. They do not record the moisture or stroke order of a watercolor. A finished fur painting also shows values, but not the moisture or stroke order that produced them.

Use miniature studies to isolate an uncertain shape, edge or material behavior. Keep their physical dimensions and simulation cell density comparable to the planned passage. Check both the paint behavior and how the study fits the whole painting.

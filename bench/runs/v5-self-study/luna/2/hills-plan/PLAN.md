# Hills — V5 try 2 plan

## Read of the crop

This is a fully wooded hillside with interleaved canopy clumps, sloping lime-green clearings, and several pale trails. There is no sky or paper border; the crop is painted edge to edge. At thumbnail size the value design is four steps: pale warm paths and sunlit grass; a broader yellow-green slope; olive and blue-green canopy; then a few deep cool tree pockets. The central winding path and its bright shoulder are the focal point. The paths are pale painted shapes, not white outlines; only the crop's brightest near-paper flecks remain bare paper. I use no rectangular reserves or family cuts, because the paths and tree boundaries meander across the whole crop.

## Regions and passage choices

- **Tree canopy, throughout the crop:** This is a wash question for the masses. On dry paper, lay connected olive and blue-green planes with the broad physical brush selected for each W15 passage. The source's individual leaf flecks are only about 1–2 target pixels (4–8 output cells at 1224 px), so they do not justify rows of small painted dots; let the fitted broken silhouette and a few later sepia marks carry that texture. Keep the crown edge hard but irregular against the grass, with lost edges only where neighboring shadows join. For each mark, check the footprint and its neighbors for the required dry state; if any contact is damp, wait or move elsewhere.
- **Lit hillsides and ridges:** These are broad value planes, not blades of grass. Use the light wash on dry paper with a flowing, medium pigment load, then the two middle passages on fully dry paper with connected broad sweeps. A crisp irregular edge defines a ridge against the canopy; soften one turning edge where the slope rolls away. The W15 brush-size choice makes the brush follow each connected plane instead of repeating a small fixed mark. Do not add individual grass strokes at this crop scale.
- **Winding tracks and narrow light cuts:** Keep their centers pale cream/ochre as painted planes; retain the true near-paper highlights as paper. Preserve the track with negative space during the green passages. Use one clear edge against canopy and let the grassy edge break into the slope. No ink runs through the pale centers.
- **Deep tree pockets:** Mix cool shadow color from ultramarine, cerulean and sap green, warmed by sienna where the target turns olive. Glaze connected shadow support across adjacent crown and slope shapes only where the target shows a joined dark mass. Apply it on a dry sheet at the final two value steps, with the reduced dark gain. The darkest cores remain paint rather than ink fills.

## Drawing and layer order

1. **Opening drawing on dry paper:** Five restrained sepia gestures: three short trail guides and two broken canopy/ridge cues. Their narrow widths and light pressure establish the central flow without enclosing every road or tree. Check the actual opening sheet before washing; if a line reads as a dark contour or crosses a light path, reduce it before the first wash.
2. **Light foundation:** Apply the pale warm and yellow-green local colors. Use a flowing, medium load (the value plan's wet-on-dry load is 0.62), and keep the sheet dry under each new footprint. This light pass spans the painted crop, but it should not make the whole scene read as one flat tint: the central path must stay warm and legible against the pale slope, and the tree masses should not yet turn dark. Allow ordinary full drying between passages.
3. **Two middle passages:** Once dry, build the broad yellow-green slopes and the connected olive/cool-green canopy with the two smoothed middle layers. Use medium, controlled loads; keep clear, crisp ridge and path edges, and soften only a chosen turning edge with clean water. W15 chooses a broad brush for large connected planes and a smaller round only when the fitted shape is genuinely small. Check measured paper state across each footprint before every stroke; wait if its required dry state is absent.
4. **Two final shadow steps:** After the middle paint is fully dry, add the connected cool shadow glaze and then the few deepest accents. These are dry-paper glazes (the planned water load is 0.45), not a dark charge into the light wash. They may bridge neighboring canopy and hillside masks when the target shadow connects, but leave the path and lime ridge open. The first dark checkpoint should frame the central path, not surround it.
5. **Closing ink on dry paint:** Use the selected, spatially distributed target-supported sepia accents on the canopy creases and trail turns. Keep them subordinate to painted darks and away from the cream path centers. The stroke file is deliberately capped at twenty short gestures; there is no hatch field or ink fill.

The paper-state check belongs to the executor before every stroke, including the opening and closing drawing. All wash and glaze footprints require dry paper at their start; a wet overlap is allowed only within the same connected passage. If a footprint or its neighbors are not dry, wait or work on another passage. No step relies on a timer or an assumed moisture reading.

## What changed after try 1

My self-review and the judges agree that try 1's jagged mosaic lost the connected hillside planes, over-darkened cool tree pockets, and made the trails too stark. I also accept the progression critique: even though the values got darker in order, the whole crop appeared faintly at the first judged stage and sharpened together. Try 1's ink-and-wash profile imposed a pale foundation across nearly the entire crop. For this landscape, I changed to the precise-watercolor value profile so the hills and canopy build as broad light-to-dark planes, then kept the requested loose ink-and-wash character in a light, selective sepia drawing layer. I changed to W15 passage brushes, reduced the middle wash count from four to two, smoothed those broad layers, returned dark gain and cool gain to 1.0, and widened the green/blue/warm palette with sienna and cerulean. I rejected a general “dark before light” diagnosis: the observed sequence already began light; the needed change is local form-building and restraint, not reversing the order. I also rejected adding more tracing ink, since that would emphasize edges without repairing the paint masses.

## Checkpoints and failure signs

- **Opening:** only a few light structural cues are present. A contour network, a line through the path center, or a linework-heavy drawing means the opening set is too strong.
- **After the first wash:** light warm tracks and the chartreuse slope are visible, with the automatic near-paper flecks intact and no dark frame. The full crop is painted, but its light and warm shapes remain distinct. A single uniform tint, white cutout roads, or missing lime planes would repeat try 1's early failure.
- **After the middle layers:** the broad slope and canopy read as connected forms, with irregular hard silhouettes and a few softened turns. Jagged chips, separate equal dots, repeated stripe bands, or a whole-sheet haze mean this layer needs correction before the shadows.
- **After the shadow glaze:** cool darks frame the center and join only where the reference joins them. Black-looking fills, detached dark islands, or lost trails are failures.
- **Closing:** a few narrow sepia accents clarify crown breaks and trail turns on dry paint. Heavy tracing or ink used to rescue soft paint is a failure.

Judge the result first at thumbnail size for the four value groups and central winding trail, then at native width for the broken canopy edges and line weight. A target feature below four output cells stays texture or selective ink; it is not enlarged into a row of painted dots.

## Live revision after pass 48

At the pass-48 review, the canopy and hillside read as dark disconnected khaki and blue chips, and the sunlit slope had lost the target's bright yellow-green. This matched the planned failure signs for a broken middle layer, so I changed the palette to hansa, sap green, ultramarine and cerulean, dropping the extra earth and sepia wash pigments. I lowered both dark and cool gain from 1.0 to 0.7 to keep the shadows subordinate. The revised plan passed admission. After re-planning, the painter returned to the before-first-wash boundary with zero brush strokes and a shorter 41-pass ladder, so the earlier sheet did not carry forward in practice. I continued with this revised palette and value restraint from the opening drawing.
